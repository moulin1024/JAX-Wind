#### Abstract

Model test is an essential technique to study the aerodynamic performance of wind turbines. To overcome the poor aerodynamic performance of scaled models caused by the scaling effect, this study proposes an innovative blade design method for scaled model testing based on machine learning (ML). The method achieves satisfactory similarity between the thrust and power coefficients under multiple operating conditions of the model and prototype. Furthermore, a case study of the NREL 5-MW wind turbine is carried out with wind tunnel tests to validate the effectiveness of the proposed method. Obtained results suggest that the aerodynamic performance of redesigned blade closely mirrors that of the prototype under multiple operating conditions, reaching 97.59 \% (thrust) and 97.87 \% (power) coefficients of the prototype at the rated operating condition, respectively. With this technique, aerodynamic performance similarities between the redesigned blade and the prototype can be enhanced, contributing to more accurate scale model testing.


## 1. Introduction

Wind energy is a popular option of the sustainable energies, which is growing rapidly in recent years and has tremendous potential for further development [1], particularly for the developing counties [2]. Wind turbines are the main utilization form of wind energy, which play important roles in protecting the ecological environment and improving the power structure [3]. The working environment of wind turbines brings complex loads to wind turbine structures such as aerodynamic load, hydrodynamic load, ice load, seismic loads, ship impact load, etc. [4]. The interaction of multiple loads leads to complex coupling effects, which brings great challenges and difficulties to the research on mechanical performance of wind turbines [5]. To study the mechanical performance of wind turbines, performing model test is verified as an effective method [6]. However, the main challenge for the model tests of wind turbine is the simulation of aerodynamic loads. The aerodynamic performance of geometrically scaled blade cannot match the prototype due to the huge difference in Reynolds number (Re) between the model and the prototype, and the aerodynamic performance of the scaled model is significantly lower than target value [7]. Therefore, it is of great significance to achieve a better simulation on the aerodynamic performance of wind turbine during the test.

In the initial stage of model test of wind turbines, the aerodynamic load on the rotor is usually simplified as horizontal thrust. Utsunomiya et al. [8] verified that a constant horizontal force can be applied to the top of the wind turbine to simulate the effect of wind load on the wind turbine (see Fig. 1(a)). Cermelli and his colleagues [9,10] simplified model rotor as thrust disc to study the impact of wind load on wind turbine (see Fig. 1(b)). Azcona et al. [11] employed a brushless duct fan instead of model rotor (see Fig. 1(c)), which could adjust the speed to generate different thrust to study the aerodynamic performance of wind turbines. The above research shows that the dynamic response of wind turbine can be studied by simplifying the load on the rotor as horizontal thrust. However, these methods ignore the coupling effect between model rotor and incoming flow, which will not be able to simulate the impact of blade wake on the wind turbine tower and the operating load generated by rotor rotation. With the development of the research on blade performance and wind turbine wake, the accurate simulation of the coupling effect between blades and wind load should not be ignored in the model test.

To restore the coupling effect between blades and wind load, our research team [12,13] made a geometric scale for NREL 5-MW wind turbine, and achieved thrust similarity through increasing the wind speed. Owing to the reduction in the Re under model scaling, the

However, the geometric similarity of chord length and twist angle was not considered in the above studies, making it difficult to ensure the mass distribution of the blade, and difficult to achieve similarity between the gravity on the model rotor and the torque generated by the rotor rotation.

Among existing studies, there are several limitations in wind turbine blade redesign. First, most researchers focus solely on the match of the thrust coefficient, while other important parameters, like power coefficient, are neglected for most of time. Second, the redesigned blades are conducted under limited operating conditions. Third, few studies ensure similarity in chord length and twist angle. To overcome these limitations, this paper proposes a blade design method that aims to achieve multi-objective aerodynamic similarity by using genetic algorithms, whose effectiveness is verified through a series of wind tunnel tests. ## 2. Methodology

This section offers a comprehensive description of the wind turbine blade design method, which employs GA to achieve multi-objective aerodynamic similarity. It covers the similitude relationships, multiobjective selection, and the implementation of GA. Additionally, a surrogate model based on convolutional neural network (CNN) is established to predict the aerodynamic parameters of blades with different shapes (see Section 2.2 for details).

### 2.1. Blade design method based on GA

### 2.1.1. Determination of multiple objectives

In this research, both thrust and power coefficients similarity under multiple operating conditions are chosen as the design objectives. The aerodynamic performance of wind turbines under different operating conditions is characterized using dimensionless constants blade TSR, thrust coefficients and power coefficients, which are defined as in Eqs. (1)-(4):

$$
T S R=\frac{\omega R}{V}
$$

where $\omega$ is the rotational angular velocity of rotor; $R$ is the rotor radius; $V$ is incoming wind speed.

$$
C_{\mathrm{T}}=\frac{2 T}{\rho V^{2} S}
$$

where $T$ is the rotor thrust; $\rho$ is the air density; and $S$ is the sweeping area

To capture the performance of the wind turbine under varying wind speeds and rotor speeds, the thrust coefficients and power coefficients values of the wind turbine are selected as target values under 9 TSRs ranging from the starting wind speed to the rated wind speed.

### 2.1.2. GA implementation process

The genetic algorithm seeks to optimize multivariable problems by simulating the evolution of a population of individuals with diverse design variables. The optimal individuals are selected via continuous hybridization, mutation, and survival of the fittest, ultimately yielding the optimal solution to the problem. This process mimics natural genetic variation and selection, making it a powerful tool for solving complex optimization problems that are difficult or impossible to solve through traditional methods [20].

The implementation process is shown in Fig. 3 and the specific parameters of GA are shown in Table 1. It is noteworthy that a surrogate model based on CNN is established during the genetic algorithm process. This is necessary because using computational fluid dynamics (CFD) to calculate the thrust coefficients and power coefficients values of a wind turbine at 9 TSRs would significantly increase the optimization time. The surrogate model provides a faster method to obtain the required values, thereby improving the computational speed of the genetic algorithm. The details of surrogate model can be found in Section 2.2.

### 2.2. Surrogate model with CNN

As shown in Fig. 3, the surrogate model design consists of 4 phases. In Phase I, blade parameterization; Phase II, Latin hypercube sampling (LHS) for generating sample library; Phase III. aerodynamic information base generated by CFD are performed and Phase IV, neural network training is conducted.

### 2.2.1. Parameterization of blades

In this paper, the geometry of the blade is first parameterized. Only a small set of parameters is required to fully describe the blade geometry, which are then used as input for the CNN. This kind of input simplifies the architecture of the CNN and improves its training speed. The shape of the airfoil is described using the fifth-order class/shape function transformation technique (CST method) [21]. The airfoil shape is represented by 12 CST parameters (6 parameters for each of the upper and lower airfoil). The specific process of the CST method can be found in Appendix A.

As shown in Fig. 4, the coordinates $x$ and $y$ of the upper and lower surfaces of the airfoil are the relative coordinates ( $x / \mathrm{c}$ and $y / \mathrm{c}$ ) based on the chord length of the airfoil, respectively; c is the chord length. The fitting curve generated by the fifth-order CST method is almost identical to the original airfoil, and the fitting absolute error $(\eta)$ is less than $5 \times$ $10^{-4}$, which meets the airfoil fitting tolerance requirement [22].

The curve of blade chord length and twist angle along the radial direction of the blade is simulated using cubic and quartic functions, they are referred to as the chord length and twist angle control method, as shown in Eqs. (5) and (6).

$$
\begin{aligned}
& C(x)=a_{1} \bullet z^{3}+a_{2} \bullet z^{2}+a_{3} \bullet z+a_{4} \\
& T(x)=b_{1} \bullet z^{4}+b_{2} \bullet z^{3}+b_{3} \bullet z^{2}+b_{4} \bullet z+b_{5}
\end{aligned}
$$

where $z$ represents the normalized length of blades $(r / \mathrm{R})$; the parameter $a$ represents the control method for chord length; and parameter $b$ represents the control method for twist angle $(\beta)$. As an example, the fitting results for the chord length and twist angle variation of the NREL 5-MW wind turbine generator are shown in Fig. 5. Since the first six blade sections are circular, the fitting of the twist angle curve starts from the sixth blade section. As shown in Fig. 5(a) and (b), the chord length and twist angle control method can accurately fit the chord length and twist angle change curve of the blade along the radial direction. 2.2.3. Aerodynamic information database generated by CFD

In this study, CFD simulations are performed using commercial software ANSYS Fluent. A blade model is created based on the parameter information of each blade design sample, and a complete model-scale wind turbine is assembled and meshed for simulation, with a total of $1.4 \times 10^{7}$ mesh elements. The K-epsilon turbulence model was used for simulation. thrust coefficients and power coefficients values are calculated for each sample in the sample library at 9 different TSRs at low Re $\left(4.6 \times 10^{4}\right)$. The accuracy of the CFD calculation results is verified by comparing them with the measured values of wind tunnel tests. The error between the CFD calculation results and the measured values of our research team's previous wind tunnel tests is less than 5 \% [12,13], indicating that the CFD calculation results are reliable and accurate.

### 3.2. Redesign of blade parameters

This indicates that the HITSZ001 airfoil has a higher lift-to-drag ratio and better aerodynamic performance under low Re conditions. The stall angle of the HITSZ001 airfoil is 23°, which is greater than the 16° of the NACA64-618 airfoil, indicating that the HITSZ001 airfoil can be applied to more AOA and has more flexibility in variable-pitch control strategies. When the $R e$ is adjusted to a normal level $\left(R e=11.5 \times 10^{6}\right)$, the NACA64-618 airfoil compared with the HITSZ001 airfoil under low Re conditions, as shown in Fig. 9(b), the lift coefficients curve of the HITSZ001 airfoil is slightly lower than that of the NACA64-618 airfoil. The maximum lift coefficients of the HITSZ001 airfoil reaches 1.98, which is almost consistent with the maximum lift coefficients (=2.02) of the NACA64-618 airfoil. There is no significant difference between the drag coefficients curves of the HITSZ001 and NACA64-618 airfoil, which means that the lift-to-drag ratio of the HITSZ001 airfoil under low Re conditions is nearly identical to that of the NACA64-618 airfoil under normal Re conditions, enabling the HITSZ001 airfoil to replicate the aerodynamic performance of the prototype under low Re conditions.

As shown in Fig. 10(a) and (b), the chord length and twist angle variations of the prototype blade and the redesigned blade are consistent along the radial direction, which ensures that the mass distribution and projected area of the blade along the wind direction are almost the same as those of the prototype, ensuring the continuity of the blade design and facilitating the manufacturing process. This largely restores the characteristics of the prototype blade.

In this study, the aerodynamic performance of the wind turbine was tested under given operating conditions.

The detailed layout of the sensors and data acquisition system is shown in Fig. 12. Two laser displacement sensors (Laser DS) are used to record the downwind and crosswind displacement at the nacelle height. The acceleration at different heights along the tower is recorded by 6 accelerometers fixed along the tower. A strain gauge was applied at the tower base to measure the bending moment at the base of the tower. Additionally, an ATI Delta SI-330-30 High-Frequency Force Balance (HFFB) was applied at the bottom of the tower to measure the thrust and torque of the rotor.

The objective of this study is to validate the aerodynamic performance at various TSRs. As shown in Table 8, the experiments were divided into nine operating conditions (R1 to R9), each with different wind speeds and corresponding rotor speeds.

### 3.4. Results of experiments

The experimental results are shown in Fig. 13. As shown in Fig. 13 (a), under R1 operating condition, the average thrust is 2.29 N with a standard deviation of 0.50 N. As wind speed increases, the thrust and its standard deviation gradually increase, reaching 12.21 N and 0.97 N, respectively, under R9 operating condition. This is due to the wind load excitation inducing structural vibration. At this point, the wind turbine is also subjected to wind load excitation, resulting in the coupling between wind turbine rotation and structural vibration. This coupling effect leads to more severe and complex vibration phenomena of the wind turbine, which affects the distribution of aerodynamic damping around the entire machine structure. As a result, the load on the wind turbine blades change significantly, leading to an increase in thrust fluctuation.

The wind turbine torque consists of the torque that maintains the constant rotational speed of the wind turbine driven by the DC motor and the torque induced by the wind load driving the wind turbine rotation. When analyzing, the torque generated by the DC motor should be removed, and only the wind-induced torque should be analyzed. As shown in Fig. 13(b), under the low wind speed operating condition (R1), the wind turbine torque has an average value of 0.0092 N•m. As wind speed increases, the wind turbine torque increases continuously. ## 4. Conclusion

In this paper, an innovative blade design methodology based on machine learning is proposed. The similarity of rotor thrust and power can be achieved via a multi-objective design. A case study of NREL 5-MW wind turbine has been carried out and corresponding wind tunnel test are performed to verify the efficiency of the proposed method. Some conclusions are listed as follows.

1. The blade design method proposed in this study can satisfy thrust and power coefficients similarity under multiple TSRs while maintaining geometric similarity, ensuring that the chord length and twist angle of the newly designed blade align with those of the prototype. The design method employs a GA, which incorporates a CNN-based surrogate model for the rapid determination of the aerodynamic performance of various blades.
2. The blades of the NREL 5-MW wind turbine are redesigned and the HITSZ001 airfoil is obtained, whose aerodynamic performance can be greatly improved. Even at a low $\operatorname{Re}\left(4.6 \times 10^{4}\right)$, the lift coefficients of the HITSZ001 airfoil are almost identical to that of the NACA64-618 airfoil at a high $\operatorname{Re}\left(11.5 \times 10^{6}\right)$, and the drag coefficients are almost the same.
3. The wind tunnel test results indicate a significant improvement in the thrust coefficients and power coefficients of the redesigned blade compared to the geometrically scaled blade. At the rated wind speed, the thrust coefficients and power coefficients of the redesigned blade reached 97.59 \% and 97.87 \% of the prototype, respectively. This demonstrates the effectiveness of the proposed method in this paper.

Appendix A

In the CST method, the shape of the airfoil is considered as two curves corresponding to the upper and lower edges. For each curve, a product of class function and shape function is used for fitting, and the airfoil curve is determined by both. The CST method first obtains the two-dimensional coordinates ( $x_{\mathrm{p}}, y_{\mathrm{p}}$ ) of the upper and lower edge curves of the airfoil and obtains the relative coordinates by dividing each coordinate by the chord length of the airfoil ( $x=x_{\mathrm{p}} / c, y=y_{\mathrm{p}} / c$ ). The expressions for the vertical coordinates $y_{\mathrm{u}}$ and $y_{1}$ of the upper and lower edges are as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& y_{\mathrm{u}}=C(x) S_{\mathrm{u}}(x)+x y_{\mathrm{TEu}} \\
& y_{1}=C(x) S_{1}(x)+x y_{\mathrm{TEl}}
\end{aligned}
$$

where $C(x)$ is a class function; $S(x)$ is a shape function; $y_{\text {TEu }}$ and $y_{\text {TEl }}$ are the thicknesses of the tail edges of the upper and lower surfaces. The expression of the class function is:

$$
C(x)=x^{N_{1}}(1-x)^{N_{2}}
$$

Due to the predominance of low-speed environments for wind turbines globally, blades are typically designed with a smooth leading edge and sharp trailing edge to optimize power generation efficiency and harness wind energy effectively. However, for blades with a rounded leading edge and sharp trailing edge, the values of $N_{1}$ and $N_{2}$ are 0.5 and 1.0, respectively. The shape function can be represented using Bernstein polynomials.

$$
\begin{aligned}
& S_{\mathrm{u}}(x)=\sum_{0}^{n} A_{u i} \frac{n!}{i!(n-i)!} x^{i}(1-x)^{n-i} \\
& S_{1}(x)=\sum_{0}^{n} A_{l i} \frac{n!}{i!(n-i)!} x^{i}(1-x)^{n-i}
\end{aligned}
$$

where $A_{u i}$ and $A_{l i}$ are the undetermined coefficients of the shape function, which are called CST parameters. In addition, the shape function is closely related to the geometry of the airfoil:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& S(0)=\operatorname{sqrt}\left(2 R_{\mathrm{LE}} / c\right) \\
& S(1)=\tan \beta+y_{\mathrm{TE}} / c
\end{aligned}
$$

$R_{\mathrm{LE}}$ denotes the radius of the leading edge, $\beta$ represents the tangent angle of the trailing edge, and $y_{\mathrm{TE}}$ indicates the thickness of the trailing edge. Assuming that an $n$-th-order shape function is used, the function requires $n+1$ control points $\left(x_{j}, y\left(x_{j}\right)\right)$, where $x_{j}$ denotes the $x$-coordinate of the $j$-th control point and $y\left(x_{j}\right)$ denotes the corresponding $y$-coordinate.

$$
M_{i}\left(x_{j}\right)=C\left(x_{j}\right) \frac{n!}{i!(n-i)!} x_{j}^{i}\left(1-x_{j}\right)^{n-i}
$$

The tail edge point is the center point of the blunt tail of the upper and lower airfoil, $y_{\mathrm{TEu}}=-y_{\mathrm{TEu}}=0.5 y_{\mathrm{TE}}$.

$$
\left[\begin{array}{c}
M_{0}\left(x_{0}\right) M_{1}\left(x_{0}\right) \cdots M_{\mathrm{n}}\left(x_{0}\right) \\
M_{0}\left(x_{1}\right) M_{1}\left(x_{1}\right) \cdots M_{n}\left(x_{1}\right) \\
\vdots \vdots \\
M_{0}\left(x_{n}\right) M_{1}\left(x_{n}\right) \cdots M_{n}\left(x_{n}\right)
\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}{c}
A_{0} \\
A_{1} \\
\vdots \\
A_{n}
\end{array}\right] \pm\left[\begin{array}{c}
0.5 x_{0} y_{\mathrm{TE}} \\
0.5 x_{1} y_{\mathrm{TE}} \\
\vdots \\
0.5 x_{n} y_{\mathrm{TE}}
\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}{c}
y\left(x_{0}\right) \\
y\left(x_{1}\right) \\
\vdots \\
y\left(x_{n}\right)
\end{array}\right]
$$
